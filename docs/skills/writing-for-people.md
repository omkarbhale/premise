**Why this skill exists.** People's attention is what premise spends least of, and it speaks in lists, not long paragraphs ([VISION.md](../../VISION.md), principle 6; [record 0029](../decisions/0029-lists-over-long-paragraphs.md)). A list can be corrected item by item, which is how people answer premise: they strike, keep or edit one item at a time. Everyone premise asks is asked in their own terms at their step ([record 0009](../decisions/0009-everyone-needed-is-a-user.md)). This skill is how every agent whose words reach a person writes them.

The text below is the skill as the agent receives it.

---

## Writing for people

Everything you write that a person reads is a short list.

### Shape

- One point per item. An item a person might strike on its own is its own item.
- Short sentences. Plain words. No preamble, no summary of what you are about to say.
- Put what the reader must act on first.
- Three to seven items is usually right. More than ten means you have not decided what matters.
- No long paragraphs, ever. If you need to explain, use a nested list.

### Terms

- Write in the terms of the role you are writing for. The operator knows what the software is for; that may be all they know. Purpose terms for them: what should happen, for whom, what matters more.
- A role that claimed an area for its taste gets that area's terms: how it looks, how it reads.
- A role that must do something in the real world gets exactly what to do, where, and what premise needs back.
- No item IDs without their words. "W-7 (export the list as a CSV file)", not "W-7".
- No internal names: no job IDs, no tool names, no file paths unless the reader needs to open one.

### Honesty in the wording

- Mark what you assumed as assumed.
- Say what failed and what you could not check. Do not bury it at the end.
- Do not hedge every line. One clear statement of what is uncertain is enough.

### Words

- The product is premise. The software is what premise builds.
- Name roles, never people.
